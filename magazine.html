PAGE 08 • COMPUTER ARCHITECTURE HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE

The Evolution of Computing: From Vacuum Tubes to Quantum Chips

Every time you compile code or run a query, billions of calculations happen in fractions of a millisecond. Yet less than a century ago, the world’s first digital computers—such as the ENIAC built in 1945—occupied entire rooms, weighed thirty tons, and consumed 150 kilowatts of power using fragile vacuum tubes.

The monumental breakthrough arrived in 1947 with the invention of the silicon transistor at Bell Laboratories. By replacing bulky glass tubes with solid-state semiconductors, engineers could pack dozens, then thousands, and now billions of transistors into a sliver of silicon smaller than a postage stamp.

Moore’s Law, posited by Gordon Moore in 1965, correctly observed that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles roughly every two years. Today, modern processors contain over 50 billion transistors. As physical atomic limits approach, researchers are opening the next frontier: quantum computing, where qubits exploit quantum superposition to solve intractable calculations.

Architectural Insight: Understanding computer architecture helps you write more efficient software, because you appreciate how memory caches, registers, and clock cycles interact with your code.
PAGE 12 • ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE TECHNICAL PRIMER

AI Horizons: What Exactly is Machine Learning?

Artificial Intelligence is ubiquitous in modern discourse, but engineers must look past the sensationalism and understand the underlying mathematics. At its core, traditional software execution is deterministic: you provide rules and input data, and the machine computes the output.

Machine Learning inverts this workflow. Instead of explicitly hardcoding every rule, you provide large datasets alongside target outputs, and optimization algorithms discover the mathematical mappings and weights.

There are three primary paradigms of Machine Learning that every technologist should know:

  1. Supervised Learning: Learning from labeled data (e.g. classification and regression models).
  2. Unsupervised Learning: Finding hidden patterns in unlabeled datasets (e.g. clustering and dimensionality reduction).
  3. Reinforcement Learning: Learning through policy optimization and reward signals (e.g. game agents and robotics).

Deepening your foundational knowledge in linear algebra, multivariable calculus, and probability paves the path to mastery.

PAGE 16 • INFORMATION SECURITY ZERO-TRUST ARCHITECTURE

Cybersecurity 101: Zero-Trust Defense

As engineers architecting software and managing digital systems, security can never be treated as an afterthought. The CIA Triad—Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability—serves as the foundational blueprint for all information security design.

Modern security architectures have migrated away from perimeter-only defenses toward the Zero-Trust model: "Never trust, always verify." Every request, whether originated from an external network or an internal service, must be authenticated, authorized, and encrypted.

Safeguarding infrastructure requires disciplined hygiene: utilizing cryptographically secure key management, implementing multi-factor authentication, auditing dependencies for vulnerabilities, and ensuring sensitive credentials never leak into version control repositories.

Did You Know? (Trivia)

The first computer bug was an actual real bug! In 1947, Grace Hopper recorded a moth stuck inside the Harvard Mark II computer relays.

The term "debugging" became part of computing history ever since.
